Pioneering a New Path at the Intersection of Technology and Art

What happens when a coder picks up a paintbrush — or when an artist writes her first line of code? One creator is finding out.

AM
By Ayat Mughal
· June 2025 · 7 min read

There is a particular kind of restlessness that strikes people who are very good at one thing and can't stop wondering about another. For years, Ayat Mughal moved confidently through the world of technology — writing code, solving systems-level problems, understanding machines. But she kept returning to a question she couldn't algorithmically resolve: what is it that art can do that software cannot?

That question, deceptively simple, eventually became the blueprint for an entirely new way of working. Today, Mughal occupies a rare position at the vanguard of a growing movement: creators who refuse to choose between the digital and the handmade, the computational and the expressive, the optimized and the beautifully impractical.

When the Algorithm Meets the Canvas

The idea that technology and art are opposed is one of the great false dichotomies of our time. Leonardo da Vinci was obsessed with engineering. Andy Warhol ran a factory. The first computer programs were written by a woman, Ada Lovelace, who described her method as "poetical science." The separation between the two was always more cultural myth than lived reality.

Still, institutions tend to enforce the divide. Schools funnel students early: STEM or humanities, code or canvas. Mughal straddled both long enough to realize the most interesting territory was in the gap between them.

"The most interesting questions live exactly where the tools run out — where the code can't tell you what to make, only how to make it."

Ayat Mughal

That tension — between the prescriptive logic of a program and the ambiguous freedom of creative practice — is where her work finds its energy. She builds generative systems that produce visual output, then intervenes by hand. She writes algorithms that don't fully determine their own results. She leaves space for accident inside systems designed to prevent it.

The Tools Are Not the Work

One of the persistent misunderstandings about tech-adjacent art is the assumption that the technology is the art — that because something was made with code, the code is what we should admire. Mughal is firm on this point: tools are means, not ends. The sophistication of the method matters only insofar as it serves the thing being made.

On creative process: Mughal's approach typically begins with a constraint — a rule she sets for herself that the work must follow — and then spends the rest of the process finding out where the rule breaks down. The violation, she says, is usually where the most interesting work lives.

This is, of course, a thoroughly artistic sensibility applied to a technical process. Rules in software engineering exist to produce reliable, predictable outcomes. Rules in art often exist to be productively broken. Mughal operates in the space between those two philosophies, using the rigidity of code to set up conditions for something genuinely unexpected to emerge.

Why This Moment Matters

We are living through an era in which the tools available to artists have never been more powerful, more accessible, or more varied. Machine learning, generative models, accessible design software, open-source creative coding libraries — the infrastructure that once required institutional resources is now available to anyone with a laptop and an internet connection.

This democratization is still working itself out. Who gets to be called a technologist? Who gets to be called an artist? Those categories are loosening, and the loosening is producing new kinds of practitioners who don't fit neatly into either box. Mughal is one of them — part of a cohort defining what creative practice looks like when the old walls come down.

The work that results isn't always legible to either camp. Technologists sometimes find it insufficiently rigorous. Art world figures sometimes find it too procedural. But that ambiguity might be precisely the point. The most generative ideas often live where established frameworks don't quite apply.

"If everyone who sees your work immediately knows what to call it, you may not have made anything new."

Ayat Mughal

What Comes Next

Mughal is characteristically reluctant to describe what she is building toward. The goal, she says, is not a destination but a practice — a way of working that stays genuinely curious, that doesn't collapse into a style or a brand or a repeatable method. The restlessness that started all of this is, she hopes, the thing she never resolves.

In an industry obsessed with scaling, optimizing, and systematizing, there is something quietly radical about insisting that the best work comes from staying in the uncomfortable middle — from refusing to let the machine think for you, and refusing to pretend the machine doesn't matter.

That is the path she is pioneering. It doesn't have a name yet. That, perhaps, is the point.
